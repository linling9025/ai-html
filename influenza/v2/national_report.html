🏥 Hospitalization Crisis: Who's Being Hit Hardest?

Hospital admissions for flu-like illness jumped by 45% this week—the largest single-week increase of the 2025-26 season. This translates to approximately 3,900 additional people seeking hospital care for severe respiratory symptoms across the country.

What this means for you: Emergency rooms and urgent care centers are experiencing increased wait times. If you develop severe symptoms (difficulty breathing, chest pain, high fever), seek medical attention immediately, but expect delays.

Who's at Greatest Risk?

Key Finding: Adults aged 65 and older account for 62% of all flu hospitalizations despite representing only 17% of the population. The hospitalization rate for seniors (89 per 100,000) is eight times higher than for adults aged 18-49.

🦠 The Viral Culprit: H3N2 Takes Over

The influenza landscape shifted dramatically this week. H3N2, a particularly aggressive influenza A subtype, now represents 68% of all positive flu samples—up from just 42% three weeks ago.

Why H3N2 matters: This subtype tends to cause more severe illness, especially in older adults and young children. Historical data shows H3N2-dominant seasons have 2-3x higher hospitalization rates compared to H1N1-dominant years.

Vaccine Effectiveness

The good news: this year's vaccine contains an H3N2 component that closely matches the circulating strain. Early estimates suggest the vaccine is 52% effective at preventing H3N2 illness requiring medical care—a significant improvement over the 2022-23 season.

🗺️ Where's the Outbreak? Southern States Drive National Surge

The national uptick in flu activity is not evenly distributed. Instead, it's being driven by an early, intense outbreak in the South that's now spreading northward.

Regional Breakdown

South (HHS Regions 4, 6): High Activity
Texas, Florida, Georgia, Louisiana, and Alabama are experiencing "Very High" ILI activity. In some counties, more than 1 in 10 healthcare visits are for flu-like symptoms.

West Coast (HHS Region 9): Moderate Activity
California and Arizona are seeing gradual increases but remain below the national average. Washington and Oregon report minimal activity.

Northeast & Midwest: Low Activity
Most states in these regions report low but increasing flu activity. Historical patterns suggest these areas typically peak 2-4 weeks after the South.

📅 How Does This Year Compare?

This season is shaping up to be more severe than average, but not record-breaking. Compared to the past 10 seasons:

  • We're currently at Week 16 of elevated activity (above baseline)
  • Peak typically occurs between Weeks 18-22 (late January to mid-February)
  • If current trends hold, this season could rival 2017-18, which saw 710,000 hospitalizations
  • However, we're still well below the severe 2022-23 "twindemic" season
Transmission Acceleration: The rate of increase (week-over-week growth) is currently at 28%, which is in the 85th percentile compared to historical seasons. This suggests the outbreak is accelerating faster than typical years.

💡 What Should You Do?

Based on the current risk level and viral characteristics, here are evidence-based recommendations:

🩺 High-Risk Individuals

  • Get vaccinated NOW if you haven't yet—it takes 2 weeks to build immunity
  • Consider wearing masks in crowded indoor spaces
  • Stock up on fever/pain medication (acetaminophen, ibuprofen)
  • Know the signs of severe illness requiring emergency care

👨‍👩‍👧‍👦 Everyone Else

  • Wash hands frequently, especially after being in public
  • Avoid close contact with sick individuals
  • Stay home if you develop fever and cough
  • Vaccination is still recommended and beneficial

🏢 If You Live in High-Activity States

  • Assume community transmission is widespread
  • Postpone non-essential gatherings if possible
  • Call ahead before visiting healthcare facilities
  • Monitor symptoms daily if exposed to sick contacts

🚨 Seek Immediate Care If You Have:

  • Difficulty breathing or shortness of breath
  • Persistent chest pain or pressure
  • Confusion or inability to wake up
  • Bluish lips or face (especially in children)
